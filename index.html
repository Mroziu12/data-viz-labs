Conflict and Human Suffering in Graphs and Numbers

NoName - Rose Delas, Mateusz Mrożek, Tomasz Błaszczak, Titouan Lefevre, Anthony Naliato

Project Overview

This webpage was created to show different types of data visualizations that help people understand both current and past conflicts around the world. By turning numbers into clear visuals, we want to make it easier to see what is really happening and how people are affected. These visualizations can highlight the terrible things that happen in many parts of the world and bring more attention to them in a simple and understandable way.

The main data used in this project comes from the ACLED (Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project) and Our World in Data. ACLED provides information about political violence, protests, and conflicts — including where and when they happen and who is involved. Our World in Data offers a wide range of global datasets, such as refugee numbers, humanitarian aid, and human development indicators.

How conflicts affect death rates and fertility

Deaths over time

Conflict-related deaths in Europe almost disappeared after the Balkan wars of the 1990s, remaining almost non-existing for two decades. The trend was broken in 2022 with Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, bringing large-scale warfare back to the continent.

Notable peaks in the 1990s

  • 1992 (Europe): Start and escalation of the wars in the former Yugoslavia—especially the Bosnian War (1992–1995) and ongoing fighting linked to the breakup of the federation—driving a continent-level spike.
  • 1995 (Europe): Climax and end-phase of the Bosnian War, including famous operations such as Operation Storm and mass murders like the Srebrenica massacre.
  • 1999 (Russia): Steep rise linked to the outbreak of the Second Chechen War after fights in Dagestan and renewed large-scale fighting in the North Caucasus.

Ukraine in 2014

The smaller increase visible around 2014 corresponds to the beginning of the conflict in eastern Ukraine, following Russia’s annexation of Crimea and the outbreak of the Donbas War between Ukrainian forces and Russian-backed separatists. Although the fighting was largely localized, it marked the return of active armed conflict to Europe after many years of peace.

Note: Values reflect the dataset’s yearly best estimates for “deaths in ongoing conflicts.” Small year-to-year fluctuations outside the highlighted periods are possible but remain comparatively low.

Deaths in ongoing conflicts in Europe

The war in Ukraine overwhelmingly dominates conflict-related deaths in Europe. In 2022 and 2023, Ukraine accounted for nearly all fatalities on the continent, and even in 2024, when the numbers slightly dropped, the majority of European conflict deaths still occurred in connection to this war.

Share of global conflict deaths by continent (100%)

On this chart we can clearly see the mentioned before periods when major conflicts erupted in Europe — the early 1990s (Balkan wars) and after 2022 (Russia’s invasion of Ukraine). Between roughly 1995 and 2020, Europe’s share of global conflict deaths remained minimal, illustrating a prolonged era of peace on the continent while most violent conflicts occurred in Africa and Asia. Onfortunately, the recent reawakening of conflict in Europe has once again drawn significant attention to the region as nearly 60% of global conflict deaths in 2024 were concentrated there.

Conflict deaths by continent in recent years

In 2022–2024, Europe recorded more conflict-related deaths than any other continent. However, the most recent data for 2025 (covering only the first half of the year) already shows a downward trend, suggesting that the situation may be improving, allowing us to look with hope toward the future.

The fertility rate across Europe, Russia, and Ukraine has shown a clear long-term decline since 1950. While families in the mid-20th century typically had around three children, by the 1980s the average number had already dropped below two.
In recent years, fertility has remained at historically low levels in all three regions. The situation in Ukraine, where birth rates have fallen even further, may also reflect the demographic impact of ongoing instability and conflict

This chart was made to see if the number of births depends on the month. It turned out that there isn’t a clear pattern - births are spread quite evenly through the year.
What we can see instead is that the total number of births keeps going down over time in all three countries. The drop is especially visible in Greece, where it likely got worse after the economic crisis.

A Global Look at Fertility and Deaths

Fertility on different continents

So here we can see that family sizes seem to differ quite a lot between continents. In Africa, women tend to have more children - maybe because of cultural traditions or different living conditions. In Europe or North America, families look smaller, which could be related to lifestyle or economic factors. Of course, these are just possibilities - we’d need to look deeper to understand what really drives those differences.

How does it look over time?

We can see that fertility has decreased in all regions, but at different speeds. Africa still has the highest fertility, while Europe and North America have stayed low for many years. Asia and Latin America show a strong decline since the 1960s, reaching much lower levels today.

Major violent periods and their causes

  • 1990–1994: Extremely high death toll caused by several catastrophic conflicts, most notably the Rwandan genocide (1994), the civil wars in Liberia, Somalia, and Sudan.
  • 2005–2009: Fightings in Sudan, the Darfur conflict, and instability in the Democratic Republic of Congo.
  • 2020–2024: Multiple concurrent crises: the Tigray war in Ethiopia, civil war in Sudan, and widespread conflicts across the Sahel region (countries localized near the southern border of sahara).

Conclusions on Europe-Africa comparison

In contrast to Europe, where decades of peace were interrupted only recently by the Russia–Ukraine war, Africa's pattern show a disturbing view. A positive trend of declining conflict deaths, visible up until the 2005–2009 period, has unfortunately reversed since then. From that point onward, the number of deaths has been consistently rising, reflecting how fragile peace remains across large parts of the continent and how new crises have emerged faster than old ones could be resolved.
